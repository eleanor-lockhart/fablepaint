# Learning to draw and animate people

A journal of teaching myself the human figure and how it moves, for Paperlight first (jointed
cut-paper figures in the boxes) and in a form that carries to other styles. Newest entry last.

Ground rules (from Ellie):

- Every asset is made by me, in code: skeletons, proportions, puppet parts, poses, motion.
  No ready-made human models (MakeHuman and the like), no motion capture files.
- References are for looking at and measuring: photographs, Muybridge's plates, films,
  anatomy tables, a mannequin I build myself in Blender if I need one, and generated images
  from OpenArt. Nothing generated by OpenArt goes into a finished piece.
- There is no hurry. Iterate, and keep the failures.

---

## Entry 0. Before any research: what I think I am doing

**The form.** Paperlight is paper and light, so the figures are paper: flat, jointed puppets
in profile, the kind Lotte Reiniger cut from card and wired at the joints, moved a pose at a
time on twos. A puppet with a dozen rigid parts and pin joints is also exactly a 2D skeleton
with forward kinematics, which I can build and reason about. The style and the mechanism are
the same thing, as they were with the print blocks.

**Why this is the hard one.** Everything so far that needed drawing skill I solved by
measuring a photograph or by hiding the subject (rule 16 of Lit Window: "hide what you cannot
draw"). A person who walks across the stage cannot be hidden, and people are the subject
viewers judge most harshly: a walk that is 5% wrong reads as wrong.

**What I think I know from memory** (to be tested): proportions of about seven and a half
heads; a walk has contact, down, passing and up positions; arms swing against the legs; the
hips rise over the planted leg. I expect, on the evidence of the heron and the ferry, that
this is a correct parts list and a wrong performance.

**Plan.**
1. Baseline: a figure and a walk from memory, no reference. Keep it.
2. Research: how Reiniger built and moved puppets; what animators say a walk is; measured
   gait (joint angles over a stride); Muybridge's plates as the thing to check against.
3. A rig in `backlot/figure.py`: skeleton in real proportions, joint angles, forward and
   inverse kinematics, poses, cycles. Review tools: pose sheets, onion skins, joint arcs, and
   a number for foot slide, because I cannot watch the walk.
4. Walk v1 from measured gait; compare with Muybridge frame by frame; fix.
5. Beyond walking: standing with weight, turning, reaching, sitting, carrying, climbing.
6. A cast: people (and animals) of the night shift, to draw again and again.
7. Put them in boxes as real paper puppets, lit and photographed.

## Entry 1. The baseline, the reading, and a rig that walks

**Study 0, from memory** (`studies/s00_memory_walk.jpg`, `.mp4`). A pictogram: a pill for a
trunk, a ball for a head, limbs of wire, legs swinging like two pendulums. The feet float and
slide, nothing has weight, and the arms are an afterthought. As with the heron: every part
present, no performance.

**Reading.**
- *Reiniger*: figures of heavy paper and card, weighted with lead, the parts joined with wire
  hinges hammered flat; leading characters were built at several sizes for long shots and
  close-ups, and the important ones had more joints. So: rigid parts, pin joints, a bigger and
  more articulated puppet when the camera comes close.
- *Animators on walking*: four positions per step. **Contact** (both feet down, legs at their
  widest, arms at their widest the other way), **down** (weight drops onto the front leg, the
  knee gives, the lowest point), **passing** (the free leg passes the standing one), **up**
  (pushed off the toes, the highest point). A march-time walk is twelve frames to a step,
  which on twos is six poses a step and twelve a stride.
- *Gait laboratories*: a foot is on the ground about 60% of the stride. Hip about 30 degrees
  forward at heel strike and 10 back at toe-off; the knee gives 15 to 20 degrees as weight
  arrives, straightens, then folds to about 60 in swing; the ankle drops the foot flat, lets
  the shin roll over it, then pushes off to about 20 degrees pointed.
- *Body proportions*: segment lengths as fractions of height (Winter, after Drillis and
  Contini): hip at 0.53, knee 0.285, ankle 0.039, shoulder 0.818, thigh 0.245, shank 0.246,
  upper arm 0.186, forearm 0.146, hand 0.108, foot 0.152.
- *Muybridge*, Animal Locomotion plate 1 (a man walking, twelve side views of one stride),
  kept on this machine as reference only. At this size it confirms the contact position
  (front heel down with the toe up, rear foot on its toes, opposite arm forward and bent)
  and shows how little the trunk leans.

**The rig** (`backlot/figure.py`). A profile skeleton in those proportions. A pose is a
dictionary of joint angles; forward kinematics gives the joints; the puppet is a dozen paper
parts with round ends at the joints, far limbs behind the body, near limbs in front.
I cannot watch a walk, so the rig reports on itself: `gait_report` gives the stride, how
much of the stride each foot is down, how far the pelvis rises and falls, and **how far the
planted foot slides**.

| | from memory | measured angles (study 1) | feet planted (study 2) |
|---|---|---|---|
| planted foot slides (statures per stride) | not measurable, feet float | 0.228 | **0.016** |
| share of stride on the ground | n/a | 52% | 65% |
| pelvis rises and falls | 0 | 0.013 | 0.034 |

**Study 1**: legs driven by the measured angle curves, body dropped onto its lowest foot. At
once a person, not a pictogram. But the number said what the stills hid: the planted foot
slid by a third of its travel. Angle curves alone do not keep a foot still.

**Study 2**: the walk turned inside out. The planted foot is fixed to the ground and rolls
heel, flat, ball; the pelvis rides over it at the height the stance knee allows; the legs are
solved backwards from the feet (two-bone inverse kinematics); the free foot is carried from
toe-off to the next heel strike. Slide fell fourteenfold. The four positions are now plainly
there in the pose sheet, and the down position has weight because the pelvis really drops.

**Not yet done.** Measuring Muybridge frame by frame against the rig. The arms are still
formula. No character: this is a mannequin. No turning, standing, sitting or acting.
