# NIGHT SERVICE opening: an outside review

*By an independent critic agent running on Claude Opus. I was not on the production and owe it nothing.*

What I looked at, and how. The five review sheets first, cold. Then the brief, the bible, the music brief and the
production log. Then the film unit by unit: about 270 individual poses, on 27 sheets of my own (consecutive runs
wherever motion mattered, crops at full scale), ten frames at full size, and two frames pulled from the encoded mp4.
Then the score in `music.py`, read as notes, plus the report, the piano roll and my own section measurements. Then
`sync.json`. Everything I made is in `out/op/critic/`, listed at the end. Pose numbers are global (pose p is at p/12 s).
Where I say *seen*, I looked at it. Where I say *inferred*, I am reasoning and could be wrong.

---

that in section 9). Everything else in the notes held up when I watched the units pose by pose.

## 2. What was meant against what arrives

The brief names six things the minute must tell someone who has read nothing. Here they are against my cold notes.

| the brief's beat | what I got cold | verdict |
|---|---|---|
| A light stays lit only while someone minds it (Orrin, 120-143) | "Lamp dark at 10.5, blazes at 11.0": a lamp that went off and on. I did not see the cause. | **Partly lands.** The idea is staged well but too fast: the nod takes 5 poses (120-125, 0.42 s), and the name card is typing on over exactly those poses (121-136). |
| A house's light goes out and walks away in a pale figure's chest, toward the boat (Marisol 192-215; Dee; Red) | "Ghosts? Passengers?" "Who the pale figures are" is first on my list of things I did not understand. I never connected the house to death. | **Fails.** The event is on pose 198, 6 poses after the cut, in a frame where the lookout cab is the brightest thing (see 5.3). |
| A power company replaces minded lights with light nobody is behind (Lang, 336-383) | "Clearest storytelling in the film: warm becomes cold. Vesper = antagonist?" | **Lands completely.** |
| Its machine runs on a cable, with a cold eye (No. 4, 384-455) | "A giant robot owned by Continuity"; the cable read as "a slanted cable/lance". | **Half lands.** The ownership is clear (the stencil); the cable is not. |
| The whales cut the cable; the eye turns green; a window opens in its chest (456-647) | "Did the robot spear an orca?" "Eyes turn GREEN. Change of side?" "Who says this?" | **The cut fails, the window half lands.** I read the snap the wrong way round: as the machine harming the whale. |
| Everyone in a row, one lit window each (648-743) | "Line-up ... the cast as lights." | **Lands.** |

So two of the six land cleanly, two land in part and two fail. The two that fail carry the plot: who the dead are,
and what the whales did. The two that land are the simplest staging in the film: one row of lights changing state
on the beat. That pattern is the lesson of this review, and section 4 returns to it.

The storyboard review (`review_storyboard.json`) predicted that a newcomer "would think it was about a ferry crew and
a giant robot". Revision 2 moved me off that. I came away with night workers, a cold-light company, ghosts and a
machine. The remaining distance is mostly execution, not boarding.

## 3. As an opening sequence

The skeleton is the right one and is used with understanding. In order: a count-in cold open (four punches on the
beats of bar 2, 24-42), a title hit on the downbeat (48), the cast introduced with name cards, a thinned
pre-chorus, a stop-time break into black and silence (384), a chorus that opens on the money shot (408-455), a
beat-cut montage (552-599), a half-time breath (600-647), a lineup (648-695) and the title again as an end card
(696-743). That is the grammar of a TV-size opening, in the right proportions for 62 seconds.

**The hook in the first seconds.** It is weak in picture and has the right idea. Poses 0-23 are black except for
Tern Point, a dot of radius 2.6 px at (168, 424) lit for 3 poses at 0 and at 12. On a phone it is invisible. The
count-in that follows (24, 30, 36, 42), four windows snapping on at exactly the pixels where the four chests
will be at 48, is good opening design. It is the same move as the "3, 2, 1, let's jam" count-in of *Cowboy Bebop*,
done with light instead of a voice, and the 47 to 48 match (four dots become four chests; I measured the dots at
x = 0.36, 0.46, 0.55, 0.65 of frame width in both poses) teaches the design language without a word. Keep it.
The two seconds before it are dead air. Either start the count at 0 or make the far light something the eye can
find: a beam flick at least 8 px across.

**The cast.** Each of the four gets a wide on the place and a cut-in on the person, with a consistent card: name,
red rule, the character's own window as a glyph at the end of the rule, role underneath. The card system is good
and should stay. The introductions are also metronomic. From 8.0 to 28.0 s every shot is exactly 2 s and every cut
is on a bar line: eleven consecutive two-second shots on one template. My attention did not leave them, but nothing
in them surprises in rhythm. Real openings vary shot length inside the bar: a pickup cut on beat 4, a 1.5 s wide
against a 2.5 s cut-in. Compare the cast introductions in the *Evangelion* opening, which cut on syncopations and
flash single frames of text.

**Escalation.** The sound escalates properly (my measurements: verse -16.2 LUFS, chorus -11.7, a 4.5 LU lift). The
picture escalates in content (people, then a company, then a machine, then whales) and in cut rate (the montage at
46-50). The break at 32.0, picture to black and sound to digital silence on the same pose, is the best-built moment
in the minute (section 8).

**The chorus and the money shot.** The money shot is No. 4 against the moon (432-455), and the tilt that reaches
it lands on the minor-seventh leap of the theme at 36.0. As a poster image it is strong. As motion it strobes
(section 6.4). The real kinetic climax is the orca (480-503), the best-drawn object in the film. It is undermined by
the cable reading as a spear, so the climax says the opposite of what it means. After the snap, the chorus spends
its second half on a walk that does not read as travelling (504-551) and a montage of near-stills of things already
shown (552-599). The chorus front-loads its best material in picture exactly as it does in the music (section 7.1).

**The ending.** The lineup lighting window by window (648-684) and the end card of windows (696-743) answer the cold
open in reverse. It is a complete ending, and the seventh light (No. 4's window, warm, with the green eye above it at
684) is the one image in the film that says "the machine joined them" without a caption. The lineup itself is the
most derivative thing in the minute, but it is earned here because the windows give it an event.

**What is its own and what is borrowed.** Borrowed: the lineup, the giant silhouetted against a huge moon
(*Evangelion*, *The Big O*), the beat-cut montage, name cards with role subtitles, the royal-road chorus progression.
Its own: the person-as-one-lit-window design language; warm against cold light as politics; the ticket punch as both
plot and backbeat; the punched card that collects window-shaped holes (176, 188); the lamp that follows the
keeper's head; the heron hidden in every shot; strict screen direction (the dead and the boat right to left, the
living left to right; I checked it in Dee's queue, the house walker, Red's track, the ferry and the fins, and it
holds). The borrowed parts are borrowed competently. The own parts are what would make someone remember it.

**Would I watch episode one?** Yes, and for two reasons the minute itself gives: Lang switching six lit windows to
cold, and the line *I am told I wrote the last entry*. That is a premise and a voice. I would arrive not knowing who
the dead are, and that is the minute's most expensive omission.

## 4. Direction and storytelling

**What lands, and why.** The Lang shot (336-383) is the model for the whole film. One light source per idea (six
warm windows, each with a different head in it: bun, curls, bald, cap). One gesture, repeated on the beat (342, 348,
354, 360, 366, 372). One state change per beat that cannot be misread: warm with a person becomes white with nobody.
One sound per change (a dry tick, `music.py` line 209). And one sign that names the cause (CONTINUITY, cold-lit). A
viewer gets it on first sight because the change is large, repeated, and happens to the brightest thing in the frame.
The lineup (648-684) works by the same rule. Do this on purpose everywhere else.

**What does not land, and why.**

- *The dead.* The pale figures appear first in Dee's queue (144) with no origin. The origin shot exists (198: the
  house window goes dark and a pale figure with a whole-body halo appears at the door), but it happens 6 poses after
  a cut, at 110 px tall, in a frame whose brightest object is Marisol's cab at the right (192-215). That breaks the
  brief's own rule of one pool of light per frame: the eye goes to the cab and the event happens behind its back.
  Nothing anywhere says *going to the boat*: the passengers walk on the car deck (144-167), but the ferry (288-311)
  is never shown taking anyone on.
- *The whales.* See 5.5. In the reveal the cable reads as a thin line of cold light with lens-shaped glints
  running along it (408-431; the mp4 frame at 34.5 s shows it clearly); in the bite it is a straight grey rod with a
  white point (480-497). A thin straight shaft with a bright point is a spear. The orca
  bites it, the frame goes white with radial cracks (498), and the cold viewer concludes the whale was struck.
- *Cause and effect across units.* The cable snaps at 41.5. The eye turns green at 50.5. Between them are four
  seconds of walk and four of montage that have nothing to do with No. 4. The only link is the severed cable dangling
  from No. 4's legs in the walk, a few dark pixels at the right edge (510). The production split the No. 4 thread
  across `f1_pod` and `f2_window` for musical reasons (the chorus belongs to the cast, the half-time to the machine).
  The cost is that the film's central transformation has its cause and its effect nine seconds apart.

**Private jokes of the production.** Some cost nothing; some cost screen time.

- The heron ("nothing counts unless the heron saw it"). Hidden in every unit: on the pile in the title (72), on the
  rock (96-119), in the sea framed by the car deck's opening (144), on a snag (198), on a pile behind the orca (490), on No. 4's shoulder against the moon (444, 614-617), in the
  lineup. As a rewatch reward this is executed with real discipline and costs nothing. But its own close-up
  (312-335) gives 2 s of the minute to a character the opening never explains. Cold, I wrote "Who is the heron?"
  Keep the hidden herons; the close-up has to earn its bar or go.
- CONTINUITY as the company's name: a film-continuity joke and, from the bible's last section, the author's own
  condition. It works publicly as a cold corporate word, so it costs nothing.
- *I am told I wrote the last entry* (630-647). The most private line in the project and the best line in the film.
  It works cold as a mystery even without attribution.
- "M/V KITTIWAKE" stencilled in near-black on near-black (144-191), and the passenger in the lineup being the only
  pale figure facing right. Invisible to a first viewer. They cost nothing and need no change.

**Which finding matters more.** The Lang shot's success and the whale shot's failure come from the same variable:
whether the story event happens to the brightest, simplest shape in the frame. That is a single directing rule, and
fixing the cable and the house beat by that rule is worth more than any other storytelling change.

## 5. Design and picture

### 5.1 Composition, value, colour

**Strong.** The Lang frame (345): sign upper left, six windows across the upper third, the figure on the right third
facing into them, the heron findable on its post at right against the water. Red's wide (240-263): the signal's big
red halo, diagonal rails catching red and then green at 258, rain across everything. The ferry (300): a symmetrical
double-ender, lit decks broken into reflection, Tern Point and the heron as quiet bookends. The heron close-up (324)
for its design. The end card (720). The palette discipline (night blues, one warm light, red only for Red and the
sigil, cold white only for Continuity, green only for No. 4 set free) is consistent across all ten units, and that
is the shared kit doing its job.

**Weak: the silhouettes are not black where it matters.** Two separate causes, both measured on display values.

- *The far limbs are drawn lighter than the body.* `fg.draw_figure` takes `colours = (far limbs, body, near limbs)`,
  and the kit passes `('#243044', '#0e131c', '#0e131c')`. In the title (72) Orrin's near leg reads (11, 16, 23), his
  far leg (30, 42, 59) and the water behind (45, 63, 80). So the far leg sits a third of the way from the body to
  the water and reads as translucent: a ghost leg, double-exposed. It is visible on every figure against the water:
  the title, the walk (510), the lineup (690). Shadow theatre (Reiniger is the reference) keeps every limb
  equally black and separates them by staging, not by tint. A lighter far limb also muddies the one thing the walk
  is for: reading the legs.
- *The master's bloom washes thin figures in front of the moon.* In the walk (510), Red's torso against the moon
  reads (94, 84, 61) against a moon of (231, 227, 210). Her fill is `#0e131c`, so the finishing pass has lifted her
  about 80 levels. No. 4's broad head in the same light stays at (39, 38, 41) (444), because bloom spill reaches the
  edges of a shape and a thin shape is all edge. The brief called the walk "the brightest sustained frame of the
  minute" and "shadow theatre", and the finishing pass turns its shadows olive-grey.

**Light pools.** The kit defines how a light is painted (`C.light`) but not how its pool falls, so each hand
invented one: flat concentric discs and hard cones from the introductions animator (Orrin 120-143, Dee 144-191,
Red's halo 240-287); feathered wedges and cones from the director (552, 588); motivated edge light from the
Continuity animator (the warm spill on rivets and stencil round the chest window, 627-647). Each is consistent
inside its unit. Together they are three lighting languages in one minute (5.6).

### 5.2 Typography and titles

The title is set wide-tracked in a condensed sans, warm off-white with a glow, with the vermilion sigil to its left
(48-95). It is clean and quiet, and it reads. Two facts the studio should know.

- **Nobody chose this weight.** `common.font()` asks fontconfig for `Open Sans Condensed:style=ExtraBold`. On this
  machine `fc-match` returns `OpenSans-CondensedRegular.ttf`. Every title, card and tagline in the film is the
  Regular weight by silent fallback. It happens to look elegant. The title's weight is an accident, and on a phone a
  32 px Regular tagline in `DIM` grey-blue (72-95) is fragile.
- `C.text()` draws each glyph separately and advances by its own width plus tracking, so the font's kerning pairs are
  thrown away. With 15 px of tracking this hardly shows; in "NO. 4" (444) and "M/V" it does.

The end card does not match the title card. The sigil is 70 px at 48 and 96 px at 696, and at 696 it is butted 25
px from the N. The logbook line (630) is the same typeface sheared 13% to look handwritten. It reads as typeset
italic, not as something a machine scratched inside its own chest. The scratch strokes round it are right; the
legible line should be scratched too.

### 5.3 The cast

The design language works at every size: you can identify each character by window colour and one accessory. Orrin
has the cap and beard, Red the brimmed hat and lantern, Dee the beanie, cigarette and hi-vis bands, Marisol the
binoculars, Lang the bob and tablet, and the passenger the fedora and suitcase. Seen in the lineup (690), though,
they are one mannequin in six hats. Same lanky build, same proportions, heights from 0.92 to 1.02. The bible's
Orrin is 63 and "walks as if the ground might be wet"; Marisol is 24. Nothing in the bodies says so. A lineup lives
on silhouette variety, and this one has very little.

At cut-in size the puppet's construction shows: capsule limbs with visible joint seams and outline strokes, no hands
(132, 175, 345). On the pale passengers the seams read as mannequin panel lines, which suits them. On Orrin at 132
they read as unfinished. Lang's hands are rounded stubs, so her "tap" (341-343) is a few pixels of stub moving in
the dark; the tablet's flash does the work instead. Marisol in the cut-in is a dark blob with binoculars and no
face (216-239). Her window behind her is a flat tan card (216, 564) where the wide's cab windows glowed amber (215).
The light does not survive the cut from wide to close.

The pale passengers are the best-designed figures in the film. Light grey paper, the brightest window, hat and
suitcase. The dead in Dee's cut-in (175) are unmistakably *other*.

### 5.4 The machine

No. 4 against the moon (432-455) is a competent mecha silhouette: two tall shoulder slabs, a pentagonal head, one
long horn, a slit eye, the stencil on the chest, the heron on its shoulder. It is also generic. Every element could
belong to any mecha. The bible specifies "transmission-tower shoulders, an insulator stack for a horn". Those are the
two details that would make this machine belong to *this* show and say *power company* without the stencil. A ribbed
insulator stack is a silhouette nobody has used for a horn. Lattice shoulders against the moon would let the moon show
through the steel, which is the most beautiful thing a shadow-theatre film can do with a giant. The full body (420,
the lineup) is a barrel torso with thin bent stick arms, and it reads as a toy.

### 5.5 The cable

The bible says the cable is "as thick as a ferry's funnel". On screen it is a line. In the reveal (408-431) it has
a dark body about 12 px wide (`C.no4`, `cw = height * .009`), which vanishes against the night sky. What reads is its
cold core, 2.6 px wide (`cw * .22`), and three lens-shaped white pulses (`cw * 1.25`) sliding along it (e_no4 lines
50-61). That is a wire with glinting spear-points: see the mp4 frame at 34.5 s, and `cable.jpg`, pose 412, at full
scale. In the bite (`f1_pod`, 480-497) it is a straight 13 px rod, grey against the moon, with a white point at the
top (`cable.jpg`, pose 490). It stays dead straight while a six-tonne animal hangs from it by the teeth for ten
poses (487-497). The path is routed through the mouth, but the kink is too small to see. A rod that does not bend
under that weight has no mass, and a massless shaft with a bright point is a spear. This is the single design change
with the most story value in the film.

### 5.6 Consistency across hands

You can tell the hands apart, and in these places.

- **The props are redesigned between units.** Tern Point's lens is a tall banded lantern with a bullseye in the
  introduction (120-143) and a round disc in a square frame with a vertical bar in the montage (558-563). The fire
  finder has no brass ring in Marisol's cut-in (216-239) and has one in the montage (570-575). Dee's chad is white
  in her cut-in (174-178, the passenger's colour) and amber in the montage (588-593, Dee's colour). That last
  difference is a different statement about whose light it is.
- **Detail density.** The Continuity animator drew the orca with teeth, eye patch and saddle (484-503), rivets on
  No. 4's chest plate (620-647) and a fully modelled heron (312-335). The leads' close-ups are bare puppets. The
  supporting cast is better designed than the stars.
- **The three lighting languages** of 5.1.

None of these is a disaster. Together they are the look of a film assembled from good units rather than designed
once. The fix is a props module in `common.py` (fix 9).

## 6. Animation

### 6.1 Inventory: how much of the minute moves

By my own classification, shot by shot.

| seconds | what moves | kind |

| 14-16 | Dee's arm, punch, falling chad, holes in the card | **character** (business) |

| 20-24 | passengers walk; Red's arm swings (266-268) | crowd walk, **character** (0.3 s) |

| 28-32 | six windows switch; a stub of hand jabs | lighting |
| 32-34 | black; an eye | hold |

| 36-38 | hold; eye pulses; card | hold |

| 40-42 | orca breach, bite, snap, fall | **puppet animation**, FX |
| 42-46 | four walkers; piles slide | **character** |

| 50-54 | eye turns green; push 612-620; shutter opens | camera, lighting |
| 54-62 | windows light in a static lineup; end card | lighting, hold |

Leads articulating limbs: about 10 seconds of 62, four of them in one shot (the walk). Background walkers: about
10. The orca: 2. Camera moves and slides: about 6. The other ~34 seconds are holds, lighting changes and
twinkle. A pixel-change measure agrees (`out/op/critic/change.npy`: blur, then the fraction of pixels changing more
than 0.03 per pose). Across the film, 40% of poses change less than 0.5% of the frame. In `a_open` it is 89% and in
`h_lineup` 75%.

A mostly-still opening is legitimate: *Mushishi* holds, and *Bebop* is mostly graphics. Those holds are composed to
be looked at, and the music carries them. Here the title hold (48-95) and the lineup (648-695) are where my
attention wandered cold, and the measurement shows they are the stillest stretches. That is the evidence that
these holds are not yet designed holds. Each wants one thing that moves for a reason: a coat in wind, a cigarette
ember, a head turning toward the next window as it lights.

### 6.2 The walks

The kit's walks plant their feet, and the units honour the contract. Orrin's walk (96-119) advances by
`C.walk_speed('keeper', 230)` a pose along the slope, and in the consecutive poses (b_walk sheet) I see no skating.
The puppets have stride, pelvis lift, lean and arm swing. They have no follow-through: coats do not swing, Red's
lantern hangs plumb whatever her arm does (264-273), and heads do not settle after the step. The walks are correct
and anonymous. Orrin's "as if the ground might be wet" is not there; the per-character styles differ by a few
percent of stride.

The four-walker shot (504-551) fails for three reasons, all seen.

1. **It does not read as travelling.** The camera moves at the mean of Red and Dee (18.6 px/pose), so the walkers
   drift only slightly (Orrin back 163 px, Marisol forward 56 px over 48 poses). The moon and water are static by
   design. The only travel cue is 22-px piles sliding on a dark band. Cold, I wrote "walking in place? Suspect
   moonwalk."
2. **A bug draws a black bar behind their shins, and the bar pops.** `backlot/draw.rect` clamps `x0` at 0 but not
   `x1`: `m[..., int(max(x0, 0)):int(x1)] = 1`. In `g1_walk` the off-screen pile `k = -1` always has a negative `x1`,
   and numpy reads the negative slice from the right. The result is a black slab across y 548-640 from x = 0 to
   x = W + x1. I measured its right end at x = 1072 at pose 504, sliding left with the dock to ~930 at 510, then
   jumping past 1280 at 511-512, and so on every ~18 poses (sheets `band.jpg`, `bandjump.jpg`). It hides the
   walkers' shins and feet against the water, which the brief's own rule says is where legs must read, and it snaps
   a 350-px block on and off in the corner of the chorus. The director knew this bug. `g2_lights.boat()` guards
   against it with the comment "a rect that ends left of the frame would wrap round". The guard was not carried into
   `g1_walk` by the same hand, and the library was never fixed.
3. **The values** of 5.1. The figures in front of the moon are olive-grey, not black.

### 6.3 The gestures

- **Orrin's wake (132-136): the best piece of motion in the film.** Head down and held in near-dark (126-131). On
  132 the head jerks up and *past* level (head -11, the arms flung, `orrin_pose` 36-39: 1.0, 0.8, 0.4, 0.12 of the
  overshoot), then settles over four poses. The lamp flares to 1.9 for two poses and settles with him. The bell hits
  at 11.0. Cause, effect, overshoot, settle, sound: everything that makes a motion read is here. Do it again on
  purpose: a story beat carried by one limb against the shot's one light, with a proper overshoot, timed to a hit.
  Its flaw is the doze before it. Five poses from awake to chin-on-chest (120-125) is a flinch. A doze is a slow sag,
  a catch, then a drop, and it needs a second or more.
- **Dee's punch in the cut-in (171-188): the best-staged business.** The arm rises against the work-lamp cone
  (171-173). The punch closes (174, with a flash). A chad of light tumbles out (175-178). The passenger's card now has
  a window-shaped hole in it (176), then two (188). This is the bible's coffee can of chads made visible, and it is
  clear at 12 fps.
- **Red's swing (264-270).** Three poses of travel (266-268) and a small settle: emphatic and right for a woman who
  has been written up eleven times. The arm is a rigid tube, which happens to make her a semaphore arm. The lantern
  should swing past and settle on its bail; it hangs plumb.
- **Marisol's binoculars (203-215).** Three held keys, as briefed, with no in-betweens. Legible against the lit
  cab. The middle key reads as a hand on her chest.
- **Lang's tap (341-343).** Invisible as a gesture; carried by the tablet's flash.
- **The single worst motion: Dee's queue (144-167).** The pale passengers walk continuously through the deckhand whose
  job is to stop them. At 144, 146 and 154 a passenger is drawn over her body, and the punched passenger keeps
  walking (152-155). Nobody stops for the punch, so the punch is a flash inside a crowd. The sync check measured its
  picture accent at 0.9 times its neighbours at all four punch poses (`sync.json`), and the production log's "by
  eye the lit chad carries them" is true only of the cut-in. The cut-in that follows has the passenger standing
  still with his card out, contradicting the wide. It is the worst motion because it is the one action that defines
  the character, and the wide animates its opposite.

### 6.4 Camera, timing and spacing

The film is encoded at 12 fps (`ffprobe`: `r_frame_rate=12/1`), so every camera move is on twos too. "Shot on
twos" means drawings at 12 a second in a 24 fps film. Camera moves and effects are normally on ones precisely
because a pan on twos strobes. The e_no4 tilt slides the scene 1000 px over 24 poses with a cubic in-out. Its
per-pose steps are 0, 2, 5, 11, 18, 26, 37, 49, 63, 78, 96, **115, 115**, 96, 78 ... At 115 px a pose, a hard-edged
moon disc and leg columns will jump, not glide (*inferred*: I cannot watch it; the numbers are well past where
panning on twos is usually judged to judder). The push from eye to chest (612-620) is gentler: a push is easier to
follow than a pan. It is still the most violent camera move in the half-time, and it lands between the snare at 51.0
and the bell at 52.0 with no sound under it.

Elsewhere the timing is careful. Light hits are struck for two poses and settle (the title at 48-49, every montage
cut, the eye at 432-433). Eases are used everywhere. Things that twinkle change on twos. The orca's arc has correct
spacing (fast out of the water at 481-483, slowing toward the apex at 485-486) and falls back after the snap with
the severed end in its mouth (501-503). Its hang (487-497) has no weight, for the reason given in 5.5.

## 7. Music

Read as a score (`music.py`), checked against the report and my own section measurements.

### 7.1 Melody

**The hook is real.** A-D-E rising (5, 1, 2 in D), then the leap nobody expects: E5 to D6, a minor seventh (bars 3-4,
landing at 6.0). In the chorus (bar 18, 34.0) it is re-rhythmed with a push: A as an eighth, D on the "and" of 1, E on
the "and" of 2. Then the seventh lands on the downbeat of bar 19 (36.0) as a D6 over a C chord: the ninth, which
resolves down by step to C6, the root. That is good tune-writing. The leap lands on a dissonance that resolves, on
the bar line, with the crash and the camera arriving. The development is thought through. In bar 22 the hook comes
back in 3+3+2 over B flat, so A is the major seventh, D the third and E the sharp eleventh: a Lydian colour, the
brightest sound in the piece. In bar 23 the leap widens to a tenth (E5 to G6). In the lineup the hook is stated twice,
an octave apart (A4 D5 E5 A5 D6 E6, 54.0-56.5). The last chord is the hook stacked (D A D E A, no third, 58.0). A
listener cannot miss that this piece has one idea and keeps it.

**The verse has no tune.** Bars 5-12 (8-24 s) are eight bars of cues, each written to the picture: a stepwise
fragment for Orrin, a sinking line for the lamp, pentatonic noodling for Dee, a high arpeggio to A6 for Marisol,
three notes for Red. No two bars share a shape, so nothing builds expectation. The hook is hidden over a bar line
(bar 6 beat 4 into bar 7) where no ear will find it. In an anime opening the A-melody is a tune the chorus answers.
Here it is a sequence of illustrations.

**The pre-chorus has no melody at all.** Bar 13 has three glass tones (the hook, very softly), bar 14 one note, and
bars 15-16 only the hum and the ticks. With the break that is 8 seconds (26-34) without a tune.

**The second half of the chorus dissolves.** Bars 24-25 (46-50) alternate low and high every beat: A4 A6 C5 G6 | D5
F6 F5 E6. On paper it is a compound melody: the upper voice falls A6 G6 F6 E6, the lower rises A4 C5 D5 F5, in
contrary motion. That is clever. Played as alternating two-octave leaps on a detuned saw at quarter notes, I
*infer* it will sound like hits rather than a line. And this is the slot where an anime chorus puts its highest,
longest, most singable phrase.

### 7.2 Harmony

Verse in D Dorian on i and IV with excursions (Dm G Am C Dm G, then F over a D pedal). The chorus is the royal-road
progression the brief named (B flat, C, A minor, D minor: IV V iii vi of F), twice. That is derivative by
instruction, and it works. The lineup's bass climbs B flat, C, D (VI, VII, i) to an open fifth on A at 57.0 and
the open D chord at 58.0. That Aeolian cadence is the most anthemic thing in the piece, and it is a sound
ending. The chorus has no cadence of its own: it ends on D minor at 49.5 and steps to B flat for the half-time. That
is reasonable because the lineup carries the cadence.

The cold tone at 28-32 is 720 Hz, between F5 (698) and F sharp 5 (740): the twelfth harmonic of 60 Hz mains, out of
tune with everything the night shift plays. I *infer* this was deliberate, and it is the right idea: the other side
is not in the key. I cannot hear whether it is unpleasant or merely odd.

### 7.3 Rhythm

The title's kick and stabs fall on 1, 2.5 and 4: a tresillo (3+3+2), and it comes back as the durations of the hook
in bar 22. That is rhythmic identity. The ticket punch as the backbeat on 2 and 4 under Dee (12.5-15.5) is the best
single idea in the arrangement: diegetic sound becomes the groove. Marisol's bars swap it for a rim, Red's for a
steady D2 eighth ostinato, which suits "unimpressed". The chorus is a standard rock pattern (kick 1, 2.5, 3; snare
2, 4). The walk (bars 22-23) has a kick and a snare on every beat with open hats on the off-beats, a stomp that fits
"the night shift walking". The pulse detector found 1.0 s, not 0.5 (report). The quarter-note pulse is not strongly
marked outside the walk. That is not a fault, but it is worth knowing.

### 7.4 Arrangement and mix

The arrangement keeps the brief's rules. The night shift's notes are struck and die (pluck, picked bass, epiano
stabs, bells). Sustain belongs only to the hum and the cold tone. The bass is built with harmonics. There is a
60 ms bass dip under every kick (lines 351-352), which is the kind of detail a careful mixer adds. The break is cut
with its room (`gate`, 3 ms), so the silence is real: the production log reports -82 dB between 32.05 and 32.95 s.

My numbers: title -14.2 LUFS, verse -16.2 (both halves), pre-chorus -22.1, hum -16.0, chorus -11.7 and -12.0,
half-time -16.4, climb -13.2, end -14.9. The report gives -14.1 LUFS integrated, true peak -1.0 dB, loudness range
7.7 LU, crest 15.6 dB and centroid 850 Hz, with bands of sub -9.0, bass -5.0, low-mid -9.8, mid -4.3, presence
-11.9 and air -18.1 dB of total. Stereo correlation is 0.89, side/mid -12.3 dB. Against the genre, which is bright,
wide and dense, this mix is dark (air at -18, centroid 850 Hz) and narrow (pans within plus or minus 0.36 on every
melodic track). It is also unusually dynamic, which is a virtue for a theme that has to sit under picture. The arp
plays sixteenths in the same register as the lead (D5-D6), though 13 dB under it. One lead at a time is honoured in
count, not in register.

### 7.5 Form, and the three questions

Form: intro (4 s), title and hook (4), verse (16), thinned pre-chorus and riser (8), break (2), chorus (16),
half-time (4), climb (4), ring-out (4). For 62 seconds that is the right shape.

- **Is there a hook?** Yes: A-D-E, then the minor seventh. It is the strongest musical asset and should be kept
  exactly.
- **Is the chorus a chorus?** In function, yes: it is 4.5 LU louder than the verse, the drums enter, the hook comes
  back at full strength, the harmony lifts to the relative major. In melody it is a chorus for 8 seconds (34-42)
  and then becomes a hit list.
- **Does the music know what the picture is doing?** Too well. Every event in the brief has its sound: steps,
  punches, the lamp's bell, the glass tone, the ticks, the break, the snap, eight hits for eight cuts, six notes for
  six windows. That makes the sync exact and the song intermittent. An anime opening is normally a song the picture
  is cut to. This is a score cut to a picture. The composer was asked for both, and the measured requirements (an
  onset within 40 ms of every listed time) rewarded the second. For an opening the song matters more.

## 8. Sync and the cut

**Sync is the most accomplished craft in the film.** The sync check finds 40 of 52 points landing in both picture and
sound and none landing in neither. Every sound onset in `sync.json` is within 10 ms of its listed time, at the
checker's 5 ms resolution; the composer's own measure says 4 ms. The best passage
is 32.0-36.0. Picture cuts to black and sound to digital silence on the same pose (384), with a 28 ms breaker click.
At 33.0 the cold eye opens on a low D. At 34.0 the chorus hits on the cut to No. 4's legs. At 36.0 the tilt lands, the
eye is struck (432, k = 26) and the leap lands on its ninth. That is four seconds of picture and music built as one
thing, and it is the template for the rest.

**The misses, read properly.**

- *Picture only* (21.5, 22.0, 24.0, 26.0, 28.0). Four of these are cuts into the thinned pre-chorus, which is a
  defensible choice. One is not. At 21.5 the Board drops to green by itself (258): Continuity's first act in the
  film, and the music plays on through Red's steady ostinato with nothing marking it. Put Continuity's dry tick there
  and the automation is *heard* as the same hand that switches the windows at 28.5. That plants the antagonist seven
  seconds early for the cost of one line (fix 2).
- *Sound only* (6.0, 12.5-15.5, 27.0, 52.0). The tagline (6.0) wipes in over 7 poses; that is a soft landing and
  fine. Dee's punches: the wide's staging problem (6.3). The heron's blink (27.0): two poses of an eye, too small to
  measure and right to be small. The window at 52.0: the measurement is fooled. Its baseline (poses 619-629) includes
  the push and the shutter widening. By eye the slit at 624 lands cleanly after four dark poses (620-623).
- *Unlisted picture changes* the checker reported and nobody acted on: 123 (10.25 s, the lamp draining, mid-nod),
  420-422 (the tilt at full speed), 499 (the flash's second pose) and 613-619 (the push). Two of these, the tilt and
  the push, are the camera moves of 6.4. The checker was pointing at them.

**The cut.** Every cut falls on a bar line except inside the montage. From 8 to 28 s that makes eleven two-second
shots in a row (6.1, 3). The order of the second half splits No. 4's cause from its effect by nine seconds (4). The
montage (46-50) is well built for eye trace: minders alternate left (x 480) and right (x 800), lights always sit at
(640, 330), and every cut is struck for two poses. But it repeats eight things already shown at the point where the
minute should peak. The cut-rate climax and the story climax (41.5) are separate events, so the montage plays as a
recap. Which matters more: the montage's craft or its placement? The placement. Its eighth slot (594-599, the boat)
could carry the eye turning green instead and rejoin the No. 4 thread at the cost of one cut (fix 6).

## 9. The method

What in the way this was made produced the weaknesses above, and what I would change in the process.

**1. Review sheets sample at half a second; most of the failures are shorter than that.** The nod (5 poses), the
punch in the wide (one flash in a crowd), the black bar's pops (one pose every ~18), the tilt's 115 px steps (only
visible between consecutive poses), the house light at 198 (6 poses into a shot). The strips the whole production
reviewed are blind to all of them. I found them only on consecutive-pose sheets. *Change:* for every listed event
and every camera move, a mandatory sheet of the 12 consecutive poses round it, made by the build and reviewed with
the strips. Plus three automatic checks: maximum camera speed in px per pose; a "pop" detector for large local
changes that are not on the sync list (the sync checker already prints these as *unlisted picture changes*, so make
them a failing test rather than a printout); and, for every walk, the background's travel per pose against the
walker's.

**2. Checks by measurement verified that things happened on time, not that they were seen.** The composer passed
every number in the brief, and the result is the most literally synced and least song-like part of the work. The
sync checker counts landings. It found Dee's punch missing in picture, and the production answered "by eye the lit
chad carries them", which was true of half the shots. No check asked a reader what the minute said. *Change:* after
each assembly, give a fresh agent that has read nothing every pose at full temporal resolution, for example
consecutive-pose sheets by the second, and ask it to narrate the story. Score it against the brief's six beats. My
cold notes are exactly that test, run once. The storyboard was given this treatment before production (three
lenses, and they were good). The picture never was.

**3. A brief split among several hands drifted wherever the kit was silent.** The kit fixed the figures, the
palette, the cards, the heron, No. 4 and `light()`, and those are consistent across all ten units. It did not fix
the props (lens, finder, chad, Marisol's window) or how a pool of light falls, and those drifted three ways (5.6).
The No. 4 thread was split across two modules (`f1_pod`, `f2_window`) for musical reasons, and nobody owned the
causal link across the gap. *Change:* any object that appears in two units goes into `common.py` as a function before
production. Any story beat that spans two units gets one owner.

**4. Code-drawn puppets produce correct, anonymous motion.** The kit's walk planted feet and eased everything, which
removes the commonest amateur faults. But the puppets have no hands, no follow-through, no secondary action (the
lantern, the coats), one body type, and far limbs drawn lighter than the body. Those are kit decisions that reach
every frame. *Change:* the kit needs a secondary-action layer (pendulum props, cloth tails), two or three body
templates, black far limbs, and a bloom that respects silhouettes. Each is cheaper to build once than to compensate
for ten times.

**5. Library defects were patched locally.** `draw.rect` wraps negative coordinates. The director knew this and
guarded one call in `g2_lights`, while the same director's `g1_walk` shipped with the bug in the chorus. Likewise
`font()` silently falls back to Regular. *Change:* when anyone finds a defect in `backlot/` or `common.py`, the fix
goes in the library with a test, the same day, and the other units are re-rendered.

**6. Render at 24 fps with characters held on twos.** Camera moves, slides, rain and FX go on ones. This is a
change to `build.py` and the shot contract (pass the sub-pose time to `frame()`), not to any drawing.

**7. Write the song first, or at least its melody.** Let the composer write a 62-second song with a real A-melody, a
B-section and a chorus line that peaks in its last four bars, *then* lay the accent list over it as ornaments. As it
stands, the requirement that every listed time carry an onset made the score structural to the picture's events
instead of to its own form.

What the method got right, and should keep: a shared beat grid that let picture and music be made blind and still
land within 10 ms; a storyboard reviewed by three independent lenses before a frame was drawn (several of its
fixes are on screen and work: the eye cold then green, the window lighting in the lineup, screen direction, the
heron device); a shared kit that kept ten units in one palette; honest logs; and a sync checker that, read
properly, already pointed at the worst camera moves.

## 10. Verdict

Judged as a prototype opening by a professional standard, with no allowance for how it was made.

| heading | score | in one line |
|---|---|---|
| As an opening sequence | **5** | Right skeleton and a real ending. Dead first two seconds, a metronomic first half, a chorus that peaks early and then recaps. |
| Direction and storytelling | **5** | Two of six beats land cleanly. The two that fail are the plot. |
| Design and picture | **6** | An ownable design language and several strong frames. Silhouettes that are not black, a generic machine, a cable that reads as a spear, props that drift between hands. |
| Animation | **4** | About 10 s of lead animation. One excellent wake, one excellent punch. A walk that does not travel and carries a popping bug. Camera moves that strobe. A queue that walks through its gatekeeper. |
| Music | **6** | A genuine hook, well developed, over sound harmony and a clean, dynamic mix. A verse without a tune, a pre-chorus without a melody, a chorus that dissolves in its second half. |
| Sync and edit | **6** | Sync 8: exact, and 32-36 s is exemplary. Edit 4: two-second metronome, cause and effect nine seconds apart, montage in the wrong slot. |
| The method | **6** | The grid, the kit and the storyboard review are good practice. Review sheets and measurement were trusted to see what only looking pose by pose, or a cold reader, can see. |
| **Overall** | **5** | A strong concept and a coherent look, proven. The animation and two story beats are not yet proven. As a pitch reel it would get the show a meeting. |

The cold-viewing section is evidence, not a performance, and is not scored.

### Three things to keep

1. **The window language and its bookends.** Four lights snapping on in the dark on four punches and matching
   pixel-for-pixel to four chests on the title hit (24-48). The lineup lighting window by window, the machine's
   last (648-684). The end card of windows alone (696-743). This is the show's logo in motion. Do not touch it.
2. **The Lang shot (336-383) as the house method.** One light per idea, one gesture, one unmistakable state change on
   each beat, each with its own sound, and a sign that names the cause. Restage every other story beat by this rule.
3. **The hook and the break.** A-D-E, then the minor seventh landing on the ninth over C at 36.0, widened to a tenth
   in bar 23 and coloured Lydian in bar 22. And the stop at 32.0: black and real silence on the same pose, an eye and
   a low D at 33.0, the chorus at 34.0. Keep the punch-as-backbeat too (12.5-15.5) as a runner-up.

### Ten fixes, in order of value for effort

1. **Kill the black bar in the walk (504-551).** In `backlot/draw.py` `rect()`, clamp the far edges too:
   `m[max(int(y0),0):max(int(y1),0), max(int(x0),0):max(int(x1),0)] = 1`. Add a test with a rect wholly off the left
   edge. Re-render `g1_walk`. Minutes of work; it removes a visible fault from the chorus.
2. **Put Continuity's sound on the Board at 21.5.** In `music.py` bar 11 add `hit('tick', 21.5, .7)` and a 0.3 s touch
   of the cold 720 Hz tone. The automation of the signal is then heard as Lang's hand seven seconds before we meet
   her. One line, and the sync check's 21.5 miss closes.
3. **Make the silhouettes black.** Far limbs the same as the body (`colours=('#0e131c', '#0e131c', '#0e131c')`, or
   darker for the far side). In `C.finish`, stop bloom from spilling into figure interiors: bloom the background
   before the figures are composited, or multiply the bloom by `(1 - figure matte)`. Acceptance: Red's torso at 510
   at or below 25/255 (now 94), and no far leg lighter than the body at 72 or 690.
4. **Make the cable a cable (408-431, 456-503).** About 30-40 px thick at the f1 framing, dark (`#0b0e15`), with the
   cold pulses travelling *inside* it as light through a hose. A visible coupling at No. 4's back during the tilt.

5. **Give Orrin's doze time and the stage (120-143).** Sag the head 0 to 20 over 120-129 with ease-in while the lamp
   drains with it. A small catch at 129. The drop at 130-131. The jerk at 132 as now. Start his name card at 136, or
   move it to the wide (108-119), so nothing types over the nod.
6. **Join No. 4's cause and effect.** The cheap version: replace the eighth montage cut (594-599, the boat) with the
   eye going cold to dark to green, so the change reads as a consequence of the snap; then `f2_window` opens on the
   green eye and goes straight into the push, which gives the shutter more time. The better version: one bar of
   No. 4's head at 42.0 straight after the snap, and the walk shortened to one bar.
7. **Restage Dee's queue (144-167).** Three passengers, not seven. Each stops at the chain, holds the card out for the
   punch on 150 and 162, then passes *behind* Dee (draw order) and exits left. Nobody overlaps her body. The punch
   then lands in picture, and the wide agrees with the cut-in.
8. **Camera moves on ones.** Render at 24 fps with characters held on twos (`build.py` and the `frame()` contract).
   Until then, stretch the e_no4 tilt to 36 poses with a sine ease (peak about 44 px/pose instead of 115) and put a
   swell under the push at 51.1-51.6 in `music.py` bar 26, which is currently silent.
9. **One model per shared prop**, in `common.py`, called by every unit: the Tern Point lens (120-143 against
   558-563), the fire finder with its brass ring (add it to 216-239), the chad in one colour (I would make it the
   passenger's white, as in 174-178, since it is cut from their ticket), and Marisol's cab window lit with `C.light`

10. **Give the theme a verse and a peak.** Rewrite bars 5-12 as one four-bar tune stated twice with variation, with
    the character cues moved into fills and countermelodies in other instruments. Over bars 24-25, play the latent
    upper line A6 G6 F6 E6 as a sustained legato top voice above the hits, so the chorus ends on its highest phrase.
    Widen the image: comp and arp to about ±0.6, the octave lead double opposite the lead.

Smaller, if there is time: break the two-second grid in the introductions (a 1.5 s wide and a 2.5 s cut-in; one
pickup cut on beat 4). Make the house light at 198 happen 6 poses later and dim the cab while it does. Give each
montage minder one small move (a head lift, a focus wheel, a lantern sway). Make the logbook line scratched, not
typeset. Fix `font()` so it fails loudly instead of falling back, then choose the title's weight on purpose. Give No.
4 its insulator-stack horn and lattice shoulders. Replace the first two seconds of black with something findable.

## 11. What I could not judge, and how far that limits this review

- **I heard nothing.** I cannot judge timbre: whether the plucked lead, the detuned saw and the epiano sound good or
  cheap, whether the kick has weight, whether the hats are lost at -25 dB. Nor perceived balance or groove: the
  humanisation is a standard deviation of 1.5-10 ms and a few percent of velocity, and whether that swings or drags is for ears. Nor whether
  the hook is catchy *heard*, whether 0.9 s of digital silence at 32 s feels like a drop or a dropout, whether the
  720 Hz tone is unpleasant or merely strange, or whether bars 24-25 fuse into a melody. My music section judges
  writing and measured balance, which are real, and leaves sound quality open. That is the largest gap, because an
  opening is first of all a song.
- **I watched nothing at speed.** Strobing is inferred from per-pose displacement (115 px at 12 fps), not seen.
  Whether the 0.42 s nod reads, whether the black bar's pop is noticed, whether eye trace across the montage works,
  and how the two-second rhythm *feels* are all judged from consecutive-pose sheets. The sheets are reliable for
  what is in each pose and weak on how motion is perceived. I would expect real playback to make the strobing and
  the pop more noticeable than I have said, and the montage better.
- **I never experienced picture and sound together.** Sync is judged from onsets and frame differences, as the
  production did. Those numbers are trustworthy for timing and say nothing about whether a hit *feels* earned.
- **Display.** I read PNG frames and JPEG sheets on an unknown display. One mp4 frame (34.5 s) showed no banding in
  the dark gradients at CRF 23, but I did not check the whole encode.
- **The bible's later episodes** (what "window" means for the Linemen, what the whales do with names) I treated as
  off-screen and judged only by what the minute shows. That is the brief's own test.

How far this limits the review: the findings about story, design, values, the bug, the queue, the cable, the edit
and the score's form stand on direct evidence and would survive a screening. The findings about how the motion and
the music *feel* are inference and should be checked by the first person on the project who can watch and listen.
